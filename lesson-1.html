Lesson 1 — Saying and keeping

Step 1: your first program

Every programmer's first program, in every language, says hello to the world. Here is yours. Type it into a file called hello.nifla:

Runs right here, in your browser.


The book says it answers:
Hello, world




Now run it:


./nifla hello.nifla


and the machine answers: Hello, world.


Stop and notice what just happened, because it is the whole shape of programming: you wrote an instruction; the machine followed it exactly. say is the language's word for speaking — hand it a value and it prints the value and a new line. The words between the double quotes are text: written words kept as a value.


Notice how little ceremony there was: no setup, no compiling, no waiting — write it, run it, read what it says. That quickness is the JavaScript half of Nifla's promise, and the early lessons live there. The other half is waiting in Lesson 8, and it is worth the walk.

Step 2: keeping a value


A program that only repeats what it was told is a parrot. Programs get interesting when they keep things. let gives a value a name:





Runs right here, in your browser.




The book says it answers:

A name with a value kept in it is what programmers call a variable. Think of it as a labeled shelf: the label stays, and what stands on the shelf can change. After let, the name can be used anywhere the value could.

Step 4: numbers, and a shelf that changes


Numbers are kept the same way, and reckoned with +, -, *, and /. And a shelf's contents can be replaced with a plain =:





Runs right here, in your browser.




The book says it answers:

Read the middle line slowly: years = years + 1 takes what is on the years shelf, adds one, and puts the answer back on the same shelf. (The 10585 is a small honest lie worth noticing: 29 × 365 counts the years in days without leap days. Programs reckon exactly what you tell them to — no more, no less. Saying precisely what you mean is most of the craft.)

The ledger grows: one gift, said out loud


Here is the first piece of the course's project. A giving ledger is a little book that records gifts — who they went to, and how much, kept in exact cents so nothing is ever rounded. (A real tally this book's first reader keeps works exactly so.) Its first piece is just one gift, kept and said:

Notice the number cents was spoken into the sentence by the +, exactly as name was. That one line — a name, an amount, a sentence — is the seed of the whole ledger.

You can now…


  • write a program and run it with nifla;
  • speak any value with say;
  • keep a value on a named shelf with let, and change


  what the shelf holds with =;


  • build a sentence by joining text with +, speaking


  numbers into it as you go;


  • reckon with +, -, *, and /.


Two small things to carry forward: lines that begin with // are comments — notes to the reader that the language steps over. And a name made inside a pair of braces { } belongs to that stretch of the program and is not seen outside it. You will meet braces in the very next lesson.

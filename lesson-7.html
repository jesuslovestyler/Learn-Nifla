Lesson 7 — When things go wrong

Step 1: the two tenses of trouble

Things go wrong in two tenses. Some problems can be proven before the program runs — those are the checker's, and you met it in Lesson 5. The rest only arise along the way: a file that is not there, a division by a number that turned out to be zero. Those belong to attempt, which you borrowed in Lesson 6. Here is its full story.

Step 2: the other tense


A zero written plainly — 10 / 0 — is the other tense: the checker proves it before the program starts and refuses the whole program, and attempt never gets the chance. The two of them work together: the checker catches what can be proven ahead of time, and attempt catches the rest, as it happens. Neither is a punishment. Both are the language refusing to let a program lie about what it is doing.

Step 4: reading a refusal


Most errors you will ever meet are neither deep nor dramatic. They are small, and the language's plain words make them easy to find. This program is shown, not run — read its refusal:


let mesage = "Shalom"
say message


Line 2: I don't know a variable called 'message' yet.
You can make one with: let message = ...
Did you mean 'mesage'?


One letter out of place, and the language not only found it but guessed what you meant. That is the spirit of every refusal in this language: name the line, say the problem plainly, point at the fix. When you meet one — and you will, everyone does — read it slowly. It is almost always telling you the truth.
